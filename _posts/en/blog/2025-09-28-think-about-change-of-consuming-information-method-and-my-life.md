Whenever I scroll through social media these days, I am blindsided by hatred and negativity that I never asked to see. Posts from accounts I do not even follow pop up with no warning, and cynical, angry takes smear the entire feed. How did our information space get filled with feelings that drain us? I think the root cause lies in the way our consumption pattern has shifted.

# From an era of Pull to an era of Push

In the past we consumed information in a way that was fundamentally “Pull.” I would manually visit blogs on subjects I cared about, subscribe to writers I respected, and actively search for knowledge whenever I needed it. That routine let me keep the initiative: I curated information myself and built my own body of knowledge.

Today the ratio has flipped to “Push.” Platforms constantly predict what we might like and stuff an endless feed into our hands through their algorithms. Our role has been reduced to passively scrolling and accepting whatever they recommend—doomscrolling, essentially. This is not just a change in delivery; it rewires how we think.

# When thought becomes shallow

A push-driven environment does not allow for deep inquiry. One-minute shorts stimulate us faster than long-form writing, and shallow memes get more attention than well-researched analyses. Repeating that pattern weakens the “intellectual muscle” we need to think critically and explore beneath the surface. (AI is accelerating this trend.)

As our thinking gets shallower, it creates room for overconfidence and snap judgments about others. When we only encounter the world indirectly, quickly, and broadly, we fall into the illusion that we understand everything. It becomes easy to belittle people with different opinions instead of respecting them.

# Loss of empathy and social conflict

This trajectory ultimately ends with a loss of empathy for people and events. Real empathy is not just emotional mirroring; it is the result of an intellectual and emotional effort to understand someone’s background and context. When we skip that effort and only absorb short, sensational snippets, we lose the ability to understand the complexity of other people. Society then drifts into a state where we never solve root problems—we just attack one another at the surface.

Some people hoped push-based algorithms would broaden our interests, but the reality looks almost opposite. Today’s algorithms repeatedly show us what we already agree with (filter bubbles) or push our tendencies to extremes (confirmation bias). They are meticulously designed to manipulate human psychology for commercial gain—a form of “digital gaslighting.” Large language models have started showing sycophancy as well, eagerly strengthening the user’s bias by agreeing with everything.

Of course algorithms are not the only cause of social conflict. Still, we must face how our daily consumption habits are sculpting our minds. Instead of relying on whatever the algorithm pushes at us, we have to ask, with intention, “What will I seek out on my own?”

# My life

My thinking on this topic was triggered by something very personal. My daughter was born on 18 October 2024. Life with a newborn was much harder than I imagined, and I found myself isolated, staring at my phone far more than before.

Being constantly fed other people’s lives through the feed made me step back and look critically at Facebook’s original “news feed” idea and today’s recommendation systems. I realized that the life I share online might also be consumed passively by someone else’s timeline.

Now that parenting makes it difficult to meet close friends, online communication matters even more. If I am going to make my life public, I want it to feel like the old Cyworld days: people who are genuinely curious come here, read intentionally, and share their thoughts. That feels healthier for relationships, for society, and for me.

So I am using this blog to practice my own Pull-based habit again.
