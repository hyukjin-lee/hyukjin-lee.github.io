Someone wrote on the Sprout Discord today, “I believe the process of overcoming constrained, restrictive environments helps us grow far more than being in a completely free environment.” The comment reminded me of an old note I had been meaning to write.

Back in 2017, when I was president of my university dev club, we watched Uber engineer Taehoon Kang’s talk [“A Silicon Valley Underdog Developer.”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dzFYUrwsdvw) It convinced me that even someone from the Korean countryside could build a meaningful career, and I have followed fguy/Taehoon’s writing ever since. Among his many talks and interviews, he once spoke about “wasted years of pain,” and that phrase stuck with me.

Many developers I respect have repeated the advice, “Go through the experience of overcoming and changing oppressive environments.” I remember Jaeseong Park and Kyungeel Lee saying the same thing at Spring Camp. Because I grew up hearing that message, I internalized the idea that “a good developer does not run from a bad workplace but changes it.” I tried to live that way for years, but eventually I had to admit that my desired direction was diverging from my organization’s. That is when I learned the simple truth: **your personal growth direction has to overlap, at least partially, with the company’s direction for both sides to grow.**

Taehoon says something similar in the video below:

> I understood the advice to “change the environment,” but personally it felt like the long periods of suffering required to do that were a waste of life.

When I first heard that as a junior, I thought he was wrong. Most environments will not fit me, I reasoned, so I had better learn how to change them. Changing the environment felt like the core skill, and chasing after a “perfect company” sounded like a Blue Bird Syndrome fantasy.

I still believe in the value of changing things, but now I add a nuance: **I have to measure whether the environment I want actually benefits that company.** If the business does not revolve around high traffic, but I keep insisting on practices meant for hyperscale teams, I am not contributing—I am getting in the way. Like life itself, software engineering has no single correct answer; it depends on context.

Assume we each have a purpose, a career direction that serves that purpose, and growth goals derived from that direction. The company’s direction needs to align with yours at least a little. If the vectors are completely different, your time together may become—just as Taehoon warned—a waste for both sides. A perfect fit may not exist, but you should at least find a team that looks in a similar direction.

Still, very few people know their purpose and direction from day one. That is why the reverse approach—fighting in the environment you have, learning from it, and discovering your direction afterward—is often more realistic. **In the end, you have to know yourself first.**

Anyway, if the discomfort keeps festering until it becomes real pain, maybe it is time to leave and chase your own blue bird.
